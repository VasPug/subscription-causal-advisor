\begin{abstract}
\noindent
How do subscription businesses actually decide what to paywall and what to
charge? The evidence says: mostly they change nothing, otherwise they read
dashboards, and only rarely do they run A/B tests. We compare all three of
these real policies against a causal advisor: a method that estimates the
effect of a change from the company's own historical data, corrects for the
biases it can see, and asks for an experiment when the data cannot support
a conclusion. We score every policy inside a simulated subscription
business where the true effect of each change is known, measuring the
profit its decisions lose. Each incumbent fails somewhere. Doing nothing
forfeits every profitable change. The dashboard reading fails when the
right move looks wrong on the surface. Even the rarely used gold standard,
a three month A/B test, fails when damage arrives more slowly than the
test: it approves a harmful paywall in 97 percent of those runs, losing
\$5{,}380 per thousand users per year, worse than doing no analysis at all.
The advisor is never the worst policy in any situation and correctly asks
for an experiment when history contains no signal. Its value is avoiding
the big mistakes, and knowing when a test is truly needed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A software product with a free tier and a paid tier faces two recurring
questions. If we lock feature X behind the paid tier, do we gain upgrades or
just lose users? If we raise the price 20 percent, does revenue go up or
down? Start with how these decisions are actually made. Randomized tests
are rare in practice: even among
high-technology startups relatively few firms adopt A/B testing at all
\citep{koning2022}, and most large US retail chains do not vary prices even
across very different markets, a pattern attributed to managerial inertia
\citep{dellavigna2019}. What firms mostly do is leave things as they are,
or read a dashboard: ``users of feature X upgrade three times more often''
or ``conversion did not drop after our last price increase.'' Both readings
are confounded. Engaged users upgrade more whether or not the feature
causes it, and businesses raise prices when things are already going well,
so the change gets credit for growth it did not create. And the rare firm
that does test faces real obstacles: price tests upset customers, and
retention effects need months to show up, so a short test can look clean
and still point the wrong way.

This paper asks whether a \emph{causal advisor}
(Figure~\ref{fig:flow}) can beat this real baseline. The advisor uses the
data the company already has, adjusts for differences it can measure, and
checks whether the recommendation survives its uncertainty. If it does,
the advisor recommends the action; if not, it asks for a specific
experiment. The design follows \citet{cadahia2026}, who built a similar
gate for retail pricing.

Real data cannot answer that, because it never shows what the other choice
would have done. Following the logic of the ACIC benchmarks \citep{hahn2019},
we build a simulation where the truth is known and score every method on
profit lost, over 30 repetitions of every setting.

\section{The simulated business}
We simulate a freemium product month by month over one year: free users
bring in \$2 per month from ads, the paid tier costs \$10 per month. Each
user has an engagement level, a willingness to pay, and a hidden taste
factor no method ever observes. Engaged users use more, stay longer, and
upgrade more. Because we control the simulation, we can replay the same
world under both choices and read off the true profit difference. The
methods see only what a real company would: noisy user attributes, feature
usage, upgrades, cancellations, revenue.

\paragraph{Decision one: lock a feature behind the paywall} Feature X is
free today, and heavier users adopt it more, so adopters look great on any
dashboard even when the feature does little. A hidden-bias dial controls how
strongly the unobserved taste factor also drives adoption. Locking cuts both
ways: some users pay to unlock, others get frustrated and quit over the
following months. We run two worlds: one where the feature is mildly useful
and locking earns about \$2.70 per user per year, one where it is a core
reason people stay and locking loses about \$5.50. The naive comparison reads almost the
same in both worlds.

\paragraph{Decision two: raise the price 20 percent} The business has three
years of signup history and considers moving from \$10 to \$12. In some runs
it raised the price once before, right when the product was improving, which
poisons before/after comparisons; in others the price never changed, so
history says nothing about price sensitivity. Demand is either insensitive
(the raise earns) or sensitive (the raise loses).

\paragraph{Scoring} Each method picks an action. Its \emph{regret} is the
profit given up by choosing wrong, plus any real testing costs it chose to
pay (test users on the worse option, waiting for results), in dollars per
thousand users per year.

\section{The three methods}
All three feed their estimate into the same simple profit formula, so they
differ only in the quality of their evidence and their willingness to say
``not yet.''

\paragraph{Do nothing} Keep the current paywall and price. Per the evidence
above, this is the modal firm's actual policy, so it is the floor every
other method must beat. It needs no data and pays no testing cost; its
regret is every profitable change it forfeits.

\paragraph{Naive comparison} Take the dashboard numbers at face value:
compare adopters with non-adopters, and conversion before and after the old
price change. If the price never changed, assume demand will not react. This
is how most monetization decisions are made today.

\paragraph{A/B test} A randomized test as a busy company runs one: 10
percent of users, three months for the paywall, two months for pricing, and
the short-term result scaled to a full year. The randomization is honest;
the scaling is the risk.

\paragraph{Causal advisor} A prediction model learns patterns in the data
as it is, and the pattern ``feature X users convert three times more''
says nothing about what happens if you lock the feature. The advisor
answers the intervention question instead, in three steps, and only the
first looks like machine learning. First, it \emph{estimates the effect} by comparing
like with like: a small statistical model learns who tends to adopt the
feature, so adopters are measured against non-adopters who look the same on
everything the company can observe (for pricing, a demand model that
controls for the time trend and seasonality). Second, it works out how far
off that estimate could be from chance alone, turning the single number
into a \emph{range of plausible values}. Third, the \emph{decision rule}:
if every value in the range points to the same action, take it. If some
values say gate and others say keep, or the history simply cannot answer
the question (the price never changed), the advisor refuses and instead
describes the experiment that would settle it. This refusal step is the
defining feature, and it is a fixed rule the advisor always applies.

\paragraph{The naive comparison fails when the right move looks wrong}
Figure~\ref{fig:bias}a shows the mechanism: the raw adopter comparison
overrates the feature by roughly a factor of three at every level of the
hidden-bias dial. Convinced the feature is precious, it never locks it,
which is exactly wrong in the world where locking pays
(Figure~\ref{fig:regret}a). In pricing, the
misread elasticity (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}b) plus the ``demand won't react''
default make it raise into sensitive demand, losing up to \$5{,}446 per
thousand signups. The failure is systematic: the same confident reading
appears in worlds that need opposite answers.

\paragraph{The A/B test fails when damage is slower than the test}
In the three month paywall test, unlock revenue arrives quickly but
frustration churn has barely started. Scaled to a year, the early revenue
wins, and the test approves the harmful paywall in 97 percent of those runs,
losing \$5{,}380 per thousand users per year, worse than no analysis at all.
The pricing test is fine because conversion reacts within the window
(Figure~\ref{fig:regret}b). A short test is a bet that the effects you care
about arrive quickly.

\paragraph{The advisor is never the worst option}
Its adjustment removes about half the bias (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}), enough
to choose correctly most of the time, and it refuses to act whenever the
decision flips inside its uncertainty range, including every run where the
price had never changed. It posts the lowest overall regret in both
decisions (Table~\ref{tab:overall}), and its worst situation (\$1{,}976)
is safer than the naive comparison's (\$2{,}689) and far safer than the
A/B test's (\$5{,}380). The do-nothing policy is a serious benchmark: on the
paywall it coincides with the naive comparison, and in pricing its \$312
regret nearly matches the A/B test's \$323; the advisor beats both by
capturing the changes inertia forfeits. The advisor's own weak spot is the bias it cannot see:
its uncertainty range covers sampling noise but not hidden confounding, so
as the hidden-bias dial rises in the locking-pays world its estimate
drifts across the decision boundary and, at the strongest setting, it
gives the same wrong answer as the naive comparison.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
These numbers show a pattern inside a world we built; no real company
would measure them. We
tuned both the simulator and the methods, with no separation between test
maker and test taker \citep{hahn2019}; the A/B failure follows from the
short window and crude scale-up we chose; all methods share one simplified
profit formula; and the advisor's abstention covers only statistical
uncertainty, leaving hidden confounding unguarded, where formal
sensitivity analysis would slot in \citep{chernozhukov2018}, since even
careful adjustment can miss \citep{young2026}. Semi-synthetic replication
on real subscription data is the next step.

In short: dashboards, short tests, and doing nothing are each a gamble on
which world the business lives in, and each is disastrous somewhere. The
advisor, which adjusts for what it can measure and asks for an experiment
otherwise, was never the worst option: never the method that quietly
loses five dollars per user per year.

\section{Reproducibility, compute, and disclosure}
The simulator, methods, and scoring code live in the project repository.
All reported numbers come from one logged run of both decisions
(\texttt{4eaaadd0}); earlier runs (\texttt{9e9cde5b}, \texttt{06bd0de4})
tested a variant whose abstention also included a hidden-bias heuristic,
which abstained more and scored similarly. Runs are seeded, and true
effects come from replaying identical random draws under both choices. Everything
runs on an ordinary CPU in under five minutes, in a Python environment with
NumPy as the only dependency, rebuilt by the fixed command \texttt{bash
run.sh}. This paper was drafted by an LLM research agent (Claude, Anthropic)
operating the simulator and literature tools, with the human author
directing the question, scope, and revisions. All reported numbers are read
from logged runs.
