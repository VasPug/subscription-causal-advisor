\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Subscription businesses decide what to paywall and what to charge using naive
correlational analytics, and occasionally A/B tests. We ask when an
observational \emph{causal advisor} --- confounding-adjusted estimation plus an
explicit act/abstain-and-experiment rule --- makes better monetization
decisions than either. Because no real dataset reveals the counterfactual, we
build a synthetic freemium business with a known oracle and score three
decision-makers on \emph{policy regret in dollars} across two decisions
(gating a feature; raising price 20\%), two economic regimes each, graded
confounding, and 30 replicates. Three findings. (1)~The naive contrast fails
precisely where the profitable move is counterintuitive: confounding inflates
the apparent value of the feature ($+13$ on a true effect of ${\sim}6$
\$/user/yr), so it never gates; endogenously timed price changes make demand
look inelastic, so it raises into elastic demand. (2)~A realistically
constrained A/B test fails where damage unfolds more slowly than the test: a
3-month gate test sees unlock revenue but not compounding frustration churn,
endorsing the destructive gate in ${\sim}97\%$ of high-value-regime replicates
--- the worst method in that regime, at \$5{,}380 regret per 1{,}000 users per
year. (3)~The advisor is never the catastrophic method (worst-regime regret
\$1{,}101 vs.\ \$2{,}689 naive and \$5{,}380 A/B) and correctly abstains to an
experiment when identification fails, e.g.\ 100\% abstention when price never
varied. An unplanned corollary: abstaining \emph{into a badly designed
experiment} imports that experiment's error, so experiment design is part of
the advisor's job. We release the simulator, methods, and per-replicate data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A software-as-a-service business deciding whether to move a feature behind its
paywall, or to raise its price, typically reasons from a dashboard: ``users of
feature X convert at $3\times$ the rate of non-users'' or ``conversion did not
drop after our last price increase.'' Both statements are confounded ---
engaged users both use more features and convert more; price changes are timed
when business conditions are improving --- and both can point to decisions
that destroy profit. The textbook remedy, a randomized experiment, is often
infeasible (price tests create fairness and grandfathering problems), slow, or
quietly biased by its own duration when the outcomes that matter (retention,
lifetime value) unfold over quarters rather than weeks.

This paper evaluates a middle path we call a \emph{causal advisor}: estimate
the decision-relevant effect from the business's own observational data with
confounding adjustment, quantify what the evidence can and cannot support, and
either recommend an action or explicitly abstain and specify the experiment
that would resolve the question. Abstention-as-output follows
\citet{cadahia2026}, who built an act/wait/experiment gate for retail list
pricing; our contribution is to evaluate this design \emph{as a decision
policy} against the two incumbents --- naive analytics and the A/B test --- on
decisions where ground truth is known, including the feature-packaging
decision where confounding is strongest and, to our knowledge, no such
comparison exists.

Evaluating against ground truth requires simulation: on real data the
counterfactual is unobserved, which is why the within-study literature from
\citet{lalonde1986} to \citet{young2026} relies on rare paired
experimental--observational datasets, and why the ACIC challenges
\citep{hahn2019} evaluate on synthetic data with revealed truth. We adopt the
simulation route deliberately: it lets us dial confounding strength,
manipulate whether identification is possible at all, and --- critically ---
price every mistake in dollars via an oracle, rather than in estimator error.

\paragraph{Claim} The right question is not ``is causal inference more
accurate than an A/B test?'' (it is not, when a well-designed test is
feasible) but ``which decision policy avoids catastrophic regret across the
regimes a business cannot distinguish in advance?'' On that question the
advisor dominates: it is the only method of the three that is never the worst,
and it knows when it does not know.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Validating observational methods against experiments}
\citet{lalonde1986} initiated the within-study comparison literature;
\citet{young2026} provide the most recent industrial instance, pairing an
experimental feature rollout at Microsoft with simultaneous endogenous
adopters, and find that doubly robust machine-learning estimators recover the
experimental benchmark for one outcome but fail for another even with rich
covariates --- a warning we encode in our advisor's abstention rule.
\citet{gordon2017} compare observational estimates against large advertising
RCTs at Facebook. The ACIC data-analysis challenges \citep{hahn2019} set the
standard for evaluating causal estimators on synthetic data with targeted
selection; we follow their logic but score decisions, not estimates, which
\citet{fan2026} argue is the evaluation that matters once ranking metrics and
effect accuracy decouple.

\paragraph{Causal decision systems in industry}
\citet{chou2026} describe Netflix's production workflow automating
observational causal inference with human oversight. \citet{cadahia2026} is
closest to our pricing experiment: a Double-ML-based pricing system whose
primary output can be \textsc{wait} or \textsc{experiment} rather than a
number, validated on synthetic generators; their validation returns ``more
negative than positive verdicts'' --- notably massive over-abstention --- which
our results complement on the packaging side. Work on combining short
experiments with long-horizon observational data \citep{colnet2024} and on
deciding whether more observational data can ever resolve a decision
\citep{effectbounds2026} formalizes pieces of the advisor's abstention logic.

\paragraph{Subscription economics}
Field evidence on freemium design --- e.g.\ a 680k-user randomized trial of
free-trial duration \citep{trialduration2025} and quasi-experimental estimates
of news paywall demand effects \citep{paywalls2013} --- documents the slow,
retention-mediated dynamics that our simulator's frustration-churn mechanism
stylizes and that break short A/B tests in our results.

\section{A simulated subscription business with an oracle}
\label{sec:sim}
We simulate a freemium product month by month over a 12-month scoring horizon:
free users generate margin \$2/month, premium costs \$10/month. Each user has
latent engagement $a_i \sim \Gamma(2,1)$, hidden taste $h_i \sim
\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and willingness to pay $w_i = 2 + 3a_i + \varepsilon_i$.
Monthly, users may upgrade (logistic in $w_i$, $a_i$, $h_i$) and churn
(logistic, decreasing in experienced value and $h_i$). The oracle simulates
any policy under common random numbers, so true policy deltas and true
treatment effects are known exactly.

\subsection{Decision A: gating a feature}
Feature X is free today. Adoption is endogenous:
$\Pr(D_i{=}1) = \sigma(-1 + 0.9\,a_i + c\,h_i)$, where $c \in \{0, 0.6, 1.2,
1.8\}$ dials \emph{unobservable} confounding ($h_i$ always affects upgrade and
churn; it affects adoption only through $c$). Engagement $a_i$ confounds at
every $c$ but is observed only through noisy proxies ($a_i + \mathcal{N}(0,
0.4)$, $a_i + \mathcal{N}(0,0.8)$, plus a pure-noise covariate). Adopters
experience extra value $x_i = \tau_x(0.3 + 0.35\,a_i)$, which raises
retention. Gating X (i) adds an unlock motive to upgrade for adopters priced
within reach, and (ii) adds frustration churn $\propto x_i$ for adopters who
do not pay. With $\tau_x{=}1$ the unlock revenue wins (true gate delta
${\approx}{+}2.7$ \$/user/yr); with $\tau_x{=}8$ frustration churn wins
(${\approx}{-}5.5$ \$/user/yr). The regimes deliberately sit on opposite sides
of the decision boundary, and the naive dashboard reads the same in both.

\subsection{Decision B: raising the price 20\%}
The business has 36 months of signup cohorts (${\sim}400$/month). Conversion
at signup is logistic in price, seasonality, and a latent quality trend $q_t$
(random-walk drift $\in \{0, 0.04\}$). In half the configurations the business
once raised price $10 \to 11$, timed \emph{endogenously} at the steepest
3-month quality upswing --- the realistic worst case for before/after
reasoning; in the other half price never varied, so the elasticity is not
identified from history at all. True elasticity $b_p \in \{0.02, 0.45\}$ makes
the proposed raise $10 \to 12$ profitable (${\approx}{+}0.6$ \$/signup/yr) or
destructive (${\approx}{-}4.2$ \$/signup/yr); churn also rises mildly with
price.

\subsection{Scoring}
Every method outputs an action. With true policy delta $\Delta$ known, the
chosen action's value is $\Delta$ (act) or $0$ (status quo); \emph{regret} is
the shortfall from the better of the two, plus any experimentation costs
actually incurred (exposure of test cohorts to the worse arm, and the delay of
withholding the better policy during the test). We report mean regret in
dollars per 1{,}000 users (or signups) per year, decision accuracy, estimator
bias, and abstention rate, over 30 replicates per cell.

\section{Three decision-makers}
\label{sec:methods}
All three share the same stylized profit template mapping an effect estimate
to a gate/keep (or raise/hold) decision, so they differ only in evidence
quality and the willingness to say ``run an experiment.''

\paragraph{Naive analytics (the incumbent)} For packaging, the raw
adopter-vs-non-adopter profit contrast. For pricing, the before/after
log-conversion contrast around the historical change; if price never changed,
the ``competitor benchmark'' default of zero elasticity.

\paragraph{Causal advisor} For packaging, a doubly robust ATT estimator
(logistic propensity $\times$ outcome regression on the observed proxies,
propensities clipped to $[0.02, 0.98]$). For pricing, user-level logistic
demand with price, linear trend, and seasonal controls. The advisor
\emph{abstains} when (i) identification fails outright (no historical price
variation), or (ii) the decision flips anywhere inside its bootstrap 95\%
interval widened by a sensitivity shift --- the estimate's movement when the
single most load-bearing covariate (packaging) or the trend control (pricing)
is dropped, a cheap stand-in for formal sensitivity analysis
\citep{chernozhukov2018}. An abstaining advisor commissions the A/B test and
pays its costs.

\paragraph{Constrained A/B test} A randomized test as a business would
actually run it: 10\% of users (or two months of signups), a 3-month
(packaging) or 2-month (pricing) window, and naive $\times 4$ (resp.\ $\times
6$) extrapolation of the short-window profit delta to the annual horizon. It
pays exposure and delay costs. The randomization is real, so it is unbiased
for the \emph{short-window} effect; its failure mode is the extrapolation.

\subsection{The naive contrast fails where the profitable move is counterintuitive}
Under confounding, the adopter contrast overstates the feature's causal value
(bias $+12.9$ on a true ATT of ${\sim}6$--$8$ \$/user/yr, growing with the
hidden-confounding dial), so naive analytics concludes the feature is too
valuable to gate --- in \emph{every} replicate, including all of those where
gating is profitable (Table~\ref{tab:packaging}, top block: accuracy 0.00). In
pricing, the endogenous timing of the historical change makes demand look
inelastic (elasticity bias $-0.35$ with quality drift), and the zero-elasticity
benchmark default makes it reckless when history contains no variation:
\$2{,}860--\$5{,}446 regret per 1{,}000 signups in the elastic, no-variation
cells. The failure is systematic, not noisy: the dashboard gives the same
confident reading in regimes with opposite correct answers.

\subsection{The A/B test fails where damage is slower than the test}
The gate test's randomization is genuine, yet it endorses the destructive gate
in 97\% of high-value-regime replicates (Table~\ref{tab:packaging}): within
three months the unlock upgrades arrive but the frustration churn has not yet
compounded, and the $\times 4$ extrapolation bakes the early win in. In this
regime the experiment is \emph{worse than no analysis}: \$5{,}380 vs.\ the
naive method's accidental \$0. In pricing, where conversion responds within
the test window, the same design is accurate and cheap (Table~\ref{tab:pricing}),
paying only exposure and delay. Short tests are not a safe default; they are a
bet that outcome dynamics are fast.

\subsection{The advisor is never the catastrophic method}
Adjustment on noisy proxies roughly halves the estimation bias (e.g.\ $+5.9$
vs.\ $+12.9$ packaging; $-0.05$ vs.\ $-0.21$ pricing), which is enough to flip
most decisions to correct; the abstention rule absorbs most of the rest. The
advisor posts the best overall regret in both experiments (\$873 and \$239 per
1{,}000 per year) and, more importantly for a policy that must be chosen
\emph{before} the regime is known, its worst-regime regret (\$1{,}101) is
2.4$\times$ better than naive's (\$2{,}689) and 4.9$\times$ better than the
A/B test's (\$5{,}380). When identification fails outright --- price never
varied --- it abstains in 100\% of replicates, which we score as the correct
output: ``the evidence cannot answer this; here is the experiment.''

\subsection{Abstention inherits the quality of the experiment it defers to}
The advisor's largest residual losses are not its own estimates but the
experiments it commissions: in the high-value packaging regime with no hidden
confounding it abstains 43\% of the time, and the truncated A/B it defers to
then chooses the destructive gate. Abstention is only as safe as the
experiment design behind it --- specifying duration and surrogate outcomes is
part of the advisor's job, not a hand-off.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
These results quantify decision quality \emph{inside a world we built}, and
four caveats bound what they license. (1)~Simulator and methods were tuned by
the same authors; the ACIC standard of generator/estimator separation
\citep{hahn2019} is not met, and effect sizes (e.g.\ the frustration-churn
coefficient) were calibrated to place regimes on opposite sides of the
decision boundary. Semi-synthetic replication on real churn data is the
necessary next step. (2)~The A/B test's packaging failure is driven by our
choice of a 3-month window with naive extrapolation --- defensible as common
practice, but a longer test or a surrogate-index design would close much of
the gap, and that comparison is unrun. (3)~All methods share one stylized
profit template, so we measure estimation-plus-abstention quality, not
business-model quality; the template's experimentation costs use oracle
quantities for accounting (not for decisions). (4)~The abstention rule is a
leave-one-covariate-out heuristic, not a calibrated sensitivity analysis; the
within-study evidence of \citet{young2026} suggests formal benchmarks are
needed before trusting ``act'' verdicts in the wild.

\section{Conclusion}
On decisions a subscription business actually faces, the methods it actually
uses are regime-gambles: naive analytics is catastrophic exactly when the
profitable move is counterintuitive, and short A/B tests are catastrophic
exactly when damage is slower than the test. A causal advisor that adjusts for
what it can measure, prices what it cannot, and abstains to a \emph{specified}
experiment is the only policy in our study that is never the worst --- which,
for a business choosing a decision process before knowing its regime, is the
property that matters. The advisor's value proposition is not ``better than an
A/B test''; it is ``never the method that loses \$5 per user per year, and
honest about when testing is unavoidable.''

\section{Per-cell results}
\label{app:cells}
Full per-cell tables (every confounding level and history variant) are in the
released per-replicate data (\texttt{packaging-results.csv},
\texttt{pricing-results.csv}); headline per-cell numbers appear in the run
logs. Two cells deserve note. Packaging, $\tau_x{=}8$, $c{=}0$: the advisor's
accuracy drops to 0.60 \emph{because} it abstains 43\% of the time and the
truncated A/B it defers to is wrong (Section~5.4). Pricing, elastic demand
with no historical variation: naive accuracy is 0.00 at \$2{,}860--\$5{,}446
regret while the advisor abstains 100\% and inherits the A/B's correct verdict
at ${\sim}\$240$--\$454 --- the clearest single illustration of the thesis.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
The simulator, methods, runner, and sanity checks (oracle-sign consistency of
the shared profit template in every regime; unconfounded-recovery checks) are
in the project repository; experiments are tracked as frozen branches
(packaging baseline and pricing child), each answered by a single logged run
(IDs \texttt{9e9cde5b}, \texttt{06bd0de4}). All randomness is seeded per
replicate; the oracle uses common random numbers across policies.
