\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A subscription business faces two common decisions. Should a feature go
behind the paywall, and should the price go up? Most teams decide with simple
dashboard comparisons. Some run A/B tests. We compare both against a third
option, a causal advisor: it estimates the effect of the change from the
company's own historical data, corrects for the biases it can see, and when
the data cannot support a conclusion it refuses to answer and asks for an
experiment instead. To compare the three fairly we build a simulated
subscription business where the true effect of every change is known, and we
measure how much profit each method's decisions lose. The dashboard fails
when the right move looks wrong on the surface, losing up to \$2{,}700 per
thousand users per year. A three month A/B test fails when the damage from a
change arrives slowly: it approves a harmful paywall in 97 percent of those
runs, losing \$5{,}380 per thousand users per year, worse than doing no
analysis at all. The advisor is never the worst method in any situation, and
it correctly says ``run an experiment'' when the history contains no useful
signal. Its value is not beating A/B tests on accuracy. Its value is
avoiding the big mistakes, and knowing when a test is truly needed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Imagine a software product with a free tier and a paid tier. The team wants
to know two things. If we lock feature X behind the paid tier, do we gain
upgrades or just lose users? And if we raise the price 20 percent, does
revenue go up or down?

The usual way to answer is a dashboard. ``Users of feature X upgrade three
times more often, so the feature must be valuable.'' Or ``we raised the price
last year and conversion did not drop.'' Both readings are misleading.
Engaged users upgrade more whether or not the feature causes it. And
businesses tend to raise prices when things are already going well, so the
price change gets credit for growth it did not create.

The textbook fix is a randomized test. But price tests upset customers, and
tests of retention need a long time, because churn builds up slowly. A short
test can look clean and still point the wrong way.

This paper studies a middle path, a \emph{causal advisor}
(Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). It uses the data the company already has, adjusts
for the user differences it can measure, and stress tests its answer against
what it cannot measure. If the decision survives, it recommends an action.
If not, it asks for a specific experiment. The design follows
\citet{cadahia2026}, who built a similar gate for retail pricing. Our
question is whether this policy makes better decisions than the two things
businesses do today.

Real data cannot answer that, because it never shows what the other choice
would have done. So, following the logic of the ACIC benchmarks
\citep{hahn2019}, we build a simulation where the truth is known and score
every method on profit lost, over 30 repetitions of every setting.

\section{The simulated business}
We simulate a freemium product month by month over one year. Free users
bring in \$2 per month from ads; the paid tier costs \$10 per month. Each
simulated user has an engagement level, a willingness to pay, and a hidden
taste factor that no method ever observes. Engaged users use the product
more, stay longer, and upgrade more. Because we control the simulation, we
can replay the same world under both choices and read off the true profit
difference. The methods only see what a real company would: noisy user
attributes, feature usage, upgrades, cancellations, and revenue.

\paragraph{Decision one: lock a feature behind the paywall} Feature X is
currently free, and heavier users adopt it more, so adopters look great on
any dashboard even when the feature itself does little. A hidden-bias dial
controls how strongly the unobserved taste factor also drives adoption.
Locking the feature cuts both ways: some users pay to unlock it, others get
frustrated and quit over the following months. We run two worlds. In one the
feature is mildly useful and locking it earns about \$2.70 per user per
year. In the other it is a core reason people stay, and locking it loses
about \$5.50 per user per year. The dashboard reads almost the same in both.

\paragraph{Decision two: raise the price 20 percent} The business has three
years of signup history and considers raising the price from \$10 to \$12.
In some runs it once raised the price in the past, right when the product
was improving, which makes before and after comparisons misleading. In other
runs the price never changed, so history says nothing about price
sensitivity. Demand is either insensitive (the raise earns money) or
sensitive (the raise loses money).

\paragraph{Scoring} Each method picks an action. Since the truth is known,
its \emph{regret} is the profit it gave up by choosing wrong, plus any real
testing costs it chose to pay (test users exposed to the worse option, and
the wait for results). We report dollars lost per thousand users per year.

\section{The three methods}
All three feed their estimate into the same simple profit formula, so they
differ only in evidence quality and in their willingness to say ``not yet.''

\paragraph{Dashboard} Compare profit of feature adopters vs.\ non-adopters;
for pricing, compare conversion before and after the old change. If the
price never changed, assume demand will not react, as industry benchmarks
suggest.

\paragraph{Causal advisor} Compare adopters with statistically similar
non-adopters using the measured attributes; for pricing, fit a demand model
that controls for trend and seasonality. Then stress test: widen the
uncertainty range and re-estimate as if the most important attribute were
missing. If the decision flips anywhere in that range, do not act; request
an experiment and accept its cost.

\paragraph{A/B test} A randomized test as a busy company runs one: 10
percent of users, three months (paywall) or two months (pricing), and the
short-term result scaled to a full year. The randomization is honest; the
scaling is the risk.

\paragraph{The dashboard fails when the right move looks wrong}
Figure~\ref{fig:bias}a shows the mechanism: the raw adopter comparison
overrates the feature by roughly a factor of three, at every level of the
hidden-bias dial. Convinced the feature is precious, the dashboard never
locks it, which is exactly wrong in the world where locking is profitable
(\$2{,}689 lost per thousand users per year, Figure~\ref{fig:regret}a). In
pricing, the misread elasticity (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}b) plus the
``demand won't react'' default make it raise the price even when demand is
sensitive, losing up to \$5{,}446 per thousand signups in the worst
settings. The failure is systematic: the same confident reading appears in
worlds that need opposite answers.

\paragraph{The A/B test fails when damage is slower than the test}
In the three month paywall test, upgrade revenue from users paying to unlock
arrives quickly, but churn from frustrated users has barely started. Scaled
to a year, the early revenue wins, and the test approves the harmful paywall
in 97 percent of those runs, losing \$5{,}380 per thousand users per year.
That is worse than no analysis: the dashboard, right here by accident, loses
nothing. The pricing test has no such problem because conversion reacts
within the window (Figure~\ref{fig:regret}b). A short test is a bet that the
effects you care about arrive quickly.

\paragraph{The advisor is never the worst option}
Its adjustment removes about half the bias (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}), enough
to choose correctly most of the time, and its stress test converts many of
the remaining dangerous calls into ``run an experiment,'' including every
single run where the price had never changed. It posts the lowest overall
regret in both decisions (Table~\ref{tab:overall}), and its worst situation
(\$1{,}101) is far safer than the dashboard's (\$2{,}689) or the A/B test's
(\$5{,}380).

\paragraph{One surprise: asking for a test is only as safe as the test}
When the advisor declines the paywall question it hands the decision to the
A/B test, and in the world where locking backfires that test is usually
wrong, so the advisor inherits part of its error. Saying ``run an
experiment'' is not the end of the job: the advisor must also say how long
to run it and what to measure, or the test repeats the mistake.

\section{Limitations}
Everything here happens inside a world we built, so the numbers show the
pattern, not what a real company would measure. We tuned both the simulator
and the methods ourselves, with no separation between test maker and test
taker, which benchmarks like ACIC treat as essential \citep{hahn2019}; real
or semi-synthetic subscription data is the needed next step. The A/B test's
paywall failure follows from the short window and crude scale-up we gave it;
a longer test or smarter early indicators would close much of the gap, and
we did not run that comparison. All methods share one simplified profit
formula, so we compare evidence quality, not business models. And the
advisor's stress test is a cheap heuristic; real deployments need formal
sensitivity analysis \citep{chernozhukov2018}, since even careful adjustment
can miss, as paired experimental and observational data at Microsoft
recently showed \citep{young2026}.

\section{Conclusion}
The two methods businesses actually use are both gambles on which world they
live in. The dashboard is disastrous exactly when the profitable move looks
counterintuitive. The short A/B test is disastrous exactly when the damage
arrives more slowly than the test. The causal advisor, which adjusts for
what it can measure and asks for an experiment when the answer is not solid,
was never the worst option in our study. That is the honest pitch for this
kind of product: not ``better than an A/B test,'' but ``never the method
that quietly loses five dollars per user per year, and clear about when you
really do need to test.''

\section{Reproducibility, compute, and disclosure}
The simulator, methods, and scoring code live in the project repository.
Each experiment is a frozen branch answered by one logged run (IDs
\texttt{9e9cde5b} and \texttt{06bd0de4}). Runs are seeded, and true effects
come from replaying identical random draws under both choices. Everything
runs on an ordinary CPU in under five minutes, in a Python environment with
NumPy as the only dependency, rebuilt by the fixed command \texttt{bash
run.sh}. This paper was drafted by an LLM research agent (Claude, Anthropic)
operating the simulator and literature tools, with the human author
directing the question, scope, and revisions. All reported numbers are read
from logged runs.
